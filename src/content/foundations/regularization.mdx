## Fitting the data versus fitting the noise

A model with enough parameters can pass exactly through every training point. That is rarely what we want, because training labels contain noise and the model's real job is to predict **new** data. **Over-fitting** means memorising the noise, and **under-fitting** means being too simple to capture the signal. Regularisation is the set of tools for landing in between.

## The bias–variance decomposition

Imagine redrawing the training set many times and refitting. For squared error, the expected error at a point $x$ splits into three parts:

$$
\mathbb E\big[(y - \hat f(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb E[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big[\hat f(x)\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}
$$

Simple models have high bias: they miss the shape consistently. Flexible models have high variance: they change wildly with each new sample. The noise term $\sigma^2$ is a floor that no model can beat. Validation error traces a **U-shape** as model complexity grows.

## L2: ridge regression (weight decay)

Add the squared size of the weights to the loss:

$$
\min_{\mathbf w}\ \frac1n\|X\mathbf w - \mathbf y\|^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf w\|_2^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \mathbf w^\star = \Big(\tfrac{1}{n}X^\top X + \lambda I\Big)^{-1}\tfrac1n X^\top\mathbf y
$$

The $\lambda I$ term makes the matrix well-conditioned and pulls every coefficient smoothly towards zero. In neural networks the same penalty is called **weight decay**: each gradient step multiplies the weights by $(1 - 2\eta\lambda)$.

## L1: the lasso and sparsity

$$
\min_{\mathbf w}\ \frac{1}{2n}\|X\mathbf w - \mathbf y\|^2 + \lambda\|\mathbf w\|_1
$$

<Callout type="math" title="Why L1 produces exact zeros">

Coordinate descent updates one weight at a time. With $\rho_j$ the correlation between feature $j$ and the current residual (with $w_j$ added back), the exact minimiser is the **soft-threshold**

$$
w_j = \frac{\operatorname{sign}(\rho_j)\max(0,\ |\rho_j| - \lambda)}{\tfrac1n\|X_{:,j}\|^2}
$$

Whenever $|\rho_j| \le \lambda$ the weight is set to exactly zero. Geometrically, the L1 ball is a diamond whose corners lie on the axes, and the loss contours usually touch it at a corner.

</Callout>

## Other regularisers you will meet

- **Early stopping**: stop training when validation loss starts rising. For gradient descent on a quadratic loss it acts much like an L2 penalty.
- **Dropout**: randomly zero activations during training (the *Anatomy of a CNN* lesson), so the network cannot rely on any single unit.
- **Data augmentation**: enlarge the training set with label-preserving transformations.
- **Decoupled weight decay (AdamW)**: see *Optimisers in practice* for why L2 and weight decay differ under Adam.

<Callout type="insight" title="Choose λ with data, not with hope">

Hold out a validation set (or use cross-validation), sweep λ on a log scale, and pick the value with the lowest validation error. Report final performance on a separate **test** set you never used for choosing anything.

</Callout>

## Summary

- Expected error = bias² + variance + noise. More capacity trades bias for variance.
- L2 shrinks every coefficient, and L1 zeroes many of them.
- Regularisation strength is a hyper-parameter: pick it on validation data.
